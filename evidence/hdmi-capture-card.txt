# The HDMI output, measured with a receiver

The capture card is the first instrument in this project that can say what the
Tang's HDMI *is*, rather than whether a screen lit up.  It changed the question.

## The instrument

A MACROSILICON USB3 video capture card (`345f:2133`) on the Tang's HDMI output,
appearing as `/dev/video0` (capture) and `/dev/video1` (metadata).  It offers
YUYV, MJPEG, BGR24 and NV12 up to 4K.  Raw YUYV is the useful one, because the
point is to read levels and a compressed frame would not have them.

The tools, all in `tools/`:

```
tools/capture-hdmi.sh <out.yuv> [frames] [device] [WxH]
tools/hdmi-frames.py <raw.yuv> [W] [H]        # how many frames, of what kind
tools/hdmi-render.py <raw.yuv> <frame> [W] [H] ramp cols rows
tools/hdmi-render.py <raw.yuv> <frame> [W] [H] zoom x0 y0 x1 y1 [step]
```

## The measurement

Fifteen seconds of capture, and a separate sixty-frame run, both returned the
same shape:

```
legal black (the Tang's own video)      36 frames   about 0.6 s
the capture card's own logo            863 frames   link not synced
one torn frame                           1 frame
```

**The Tang's HDMI is not dead.**  It emits a valid TMDS signal that a receiver
locks onto and decodes, carrying legal digital black -- luma 16 throughout, which
is what a conformant source puts in active pixels.  Then, after about 0.6 s, the
link drops and does not come back until the capture is reopened.

## Two kinds of frame, and why telling them apart mattered

A frame at luma 16 everywhere is the *source's* video black.  A frame whose
background is luma **0** with bright text on it is the *card's* logo: legal video
never has 0 in the active pixels.  The first capture in this session was mostly
the second kind, and it was briefly read as the Tang displaying text.  The user
identified the logo; the luma-0 background is the tell, and `tools/hdmi-frames.py`
now checks it and says which kind a frame is.

## What it means

**The premise was wrong.**  "Its HDMI output produces no signal" has been the
project's description of this fault since the beginning, and it is not what is
happening: the output produces a signal a receiver accepts, and the *link* then
fails.  The fault is signal integrity, not absence.

**And the monitor was never the problem.**  The monitor's "flash, then no signal,
then sleep" is the same 0.6 s of lock seen from a second receiver.  A transient
flash at load time was earlier dismissed here as the monitor reacting to
configuration; measured, it is the signal itself, and the two receivers agree.

## Why the link drops, from the router's own report

This build's dedicated-routing failures, out of 947:

```
943  Failed to route net 'clk'      the ~21.5 MHz NES clock
  3  Failed to route net 'hclk5'     the 371.25 MHz TMDS bit clock
  1  Failed to route net 'clk27'
```

`clk` at 21.5 MHz is fine on fabric, and the keylink answering steadily for as
long as it has been watched proves it.  `hclk5` is not: it is the TMDS bit clock,
and three of its segments are on general fabric rather than the dedicated clock
network.  At 371 MHz that is jitter, and jitter is what makes a receiver lock and
then fall out -- which is what both receivers do.

That is the same defect the project already named -- "the route that carries the
TMDS bit clock to the serialisers is missing inside nextpnr" -- now measured from
the receiving end rather than inferred.

## Next

Compare how the **vendor's** bitstream routes `hclk5` against ours.  Its build
works on this board, so whatever it does with that net that ours does not is the
answer, and the project already carries `hclk-state.py` and `dump-routing.py` for
reading HCLK state out of an unpacked bitstream.  That is a comparison, not a
hunt.
