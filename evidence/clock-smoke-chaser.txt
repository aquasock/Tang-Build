# clock-smoke as an eight-lane chaser, and the hclk anomaly it exposed

# Design:   fpga/clock-smoke/clock_smoke.v, all eight PMOD1 lanes driven
# Built:    scripts/build-clock-smoke.sh
# Bitstream: 34,668,145 bytes
#          sha256 6552c352ba524e3f1d2415ac6e81398af3e195ea2849d882d096211fabf780e5
#          (an earlier build of the same chaser drove the lit lane LOW and is
#          0bd6341516805b1582442ef7fcfdbf6f16ad4678b5e682cccc4c9a24c275dfe9)
# Loaded:   openFPGALoader -b tangconsole, one-wire (MCU port 0403:6010), SRAM,
#           erasing and loading to 100% and reporting DONE -- after a power
#           cycle each time, which section 5 shows is not optional.
# Captured: /dev/ttyUSB1 at 115200
# Observed: the user's report on both builds: a marker travels LED 1 -> 8 in
#           order and then repeats.  Dark in the first build, lit in the second.

==================================================================
1. What the chaser is, and why it replaces the five per-clock lanes
==================================================================

All eight PMOD1 lanes are driven, one at a time, so a single marker walks the
module at 4 steps a second -- a lap takes 2 s and every lane blinks at 0.5 Hz.
The step is counted in the `sys_clk` domain, so it is independent of every PLL
in the design and cannot be confused with a clock report.

It was built for two reasons.  The first is that a travelling marker makes the
module's lane ORDER readable by eye, and a direction of travel cannot be
mistaken -- which is what turns the dock's row interleave from a sourced claim
into a measured one.  The second is that this is the first output of this
project a person can recognise, which a UART line is not.

What it gives up is the meter reading of `cnt_sys`, `cnt_27`, `cnt_hclk` and the
two lock bits that the previous version exposed on five lanes.  The UART keeps
all of that and better -- the counters are still in the design and still read by
the report, only their pins are gone -- which is why losing them costs nothing
that is not already recorded.

==================================================================
2. The lane order is confirmed on hardware
==================================================================

The chaser drives module lane n on IO0/2/4/6 for n = 0-3 and IO1/3/5/7 for
n = 4-7, i.e. straight through the dock's interleave, so lane order is what
produces an in-order walk.  The user reports the marker travelling 1 -> 2 -> ...
-> 8 and then repeating.

That is the first hardware confirmation of the interleave `PMOD-001` and
Tang-Phosphor's `pmod_slot.sv` record, and it retires the one assumption
`evidence/clock-smoke-led-and-uart.txt` section 3 rested on -- that the module's
own LEDs run in lane order.  They do: an out-of-order or jumped walk would have
shown the assumption to be false, and the walk is in order.

==================================================================
3. The polarity, and a correction to clock-smoke-led-and-uart.txt
==================================================================

The module lights when its lane is driven HIGH, not LOW.

Measured by the chaser, which is the cleaner instrument because the driven lane
is known by construction: with the lit lane driven LOW the user saw a HOLE
travelling and the other seven lit; with the lit lane driven HIGH the user saw
a lit marker travelling.  LOW therefore means dark.

`evidence/clock-smoke-led-and-uart.txt` section 3 concludes the opposite -- that
the module is active-low -- from the two PLL lock lanes being dark while their
pins were high.  That reading is now wrong somewhere, and the most likely place
is the observation rather than the pin state: `lock27` and `lock_hdmi` are
active-high and the report drives '1' for them, so lanes 2 and 5 were driven
high and, on this module, should have been LIT.  Either those two LEDs were
misread at a glance among eight, or those two lanes were not in fact driving.
What is not in doubt is the chaser's measurement, so that is what the design
follows.  This also explains the power-on state the user reported: a ball with
no design on it reads high, so an active-high module lights all eight.

The lesson is the project's own, recorded twice before and now a third time:
a claim generalised from a glance at one or two cells is not a measurement, and
the instrument that drives a known value is worth more than the one that infers
it from what is seen.

==================================================================
4. The hclk anomaly: the CLKDIV's output does not toggle in this design
==================================================================

The UART reports both PLL lock bits set and `clk27` running, and reports the
`hclk` count as exactly zero, in the same design and on the same board whose
predecessor reported 148.5 MHz.  Every capture in this session, in order:

  capture                design        load state        hclk
  uart-clocksmoke.bin    pre-chaser    after power cycle 2.9701 x sys_clk
  uart-clocksmoke2.bin   pre-chaser    after power cycle 2.9700 x sys_clk
  uart-chase.bin         chaser (hole) NO power cycle    2.5874 x sys_clk  <- discarded
  uart-chase2.bin        chaser (hole) after power cycle 0.0000 x sys_clk
  uart-chase3.bin        chaser (lit)  after power cycle 0.0000 x sys_clk

The third row is the load the user stopped: without a power cycle the board was
in an indeterminate state and its numbers are not evidence, which is why they
are marked discarded rather than counted.  Excluding it, the pattern is clean:
two pre-chaser loads measure `hclk`, and two chaser loads -- two different
builds, each loaded after its own power cycle -- do not.

What was checked in the routed netlist, rather than inferred:

  div5.HCLKIN   <- hclk5
  div5.CLKOUT   -> hclk
  hclk              clocks 25 counter flops
  cnt_hclk[12]      driven by a flip-flop whose clock net is hclk
  that bit          feeds the sampler that feeds the `hk=` field

So the design states that `hclk` must toggle and be counted, and the count is
zero.  The counter's upper bits losing their pin consumer this cycle is not the
explanation: bit 12 is the bit that is read, it is still driven by an
`hclk`-clocked flop, and nextpnr still constrains `hclk` and reports endpoints
for it.

What follows is measured: the CLKDIV's output is not toggling on silicon in
this design.  What is inferred, and is the reason this is worth recording, is
that `hclk5` reaches the CLKDIV over the general-fabric fallback after its one
`Failed to route ... using dedicated routing`, and that this fallback is
fragile enough to be broken by an unrelated change to the I/O -- adding three
more driven lanes and a small amount of `sys_clk` logic.  That inference is not
established.  What is established is that this design can measure it, and that
the symptom is machine-readable: a counter that stops, rather than a screen
that stays dark.

That matters because it is the desktop core's open candidate in miniature.
Section 18 of `desktop-clock-routing.txt` records that the working vendor build
drives lane 0 of the HCLK block from the dedicated inter-HCLK wire while ours
arrives over fabric, and section 19 leaves it open whether that or the
serialiser attribute difference is why no TMDS leaves the part.  A small design
in which a `371.25 MHz`-to-`74.25 MHz` division either works or does not, with a
UART to say which, is the instrument that question has been missing.

==================================================================
5. Loading requires a power cycle, and TOOLCHAIN.md did not say so
==================================================================

An SRAM load onto an already-running design does not take effect.  This was
measured in core-log entry 8 and interpreted there: with the board not power
cycled, a load is read as the PREVIOUS bitstream's behaviour, so a variant
appears to change nothing or to change the wrong thing.  It bit this session
anyway -- the `uart-chase.bin` row above is exactly that, and it produced a
plausible-looking 2.5874 x sys_clk that would have been written up as a finding
had the user not stopped the capture and pointed out that no reboot had
happened.

`TOOLCHAIN.md`'s board-side section documents the one-wire load and that it is
volatile, but not that a power cycle precedes every load.  That is now recorded
there, because a hazard that costs a false measurement is worth more than the
paragraph it costs to write down.

==================================================================
6. What is open
==================================================================

- Whether the chaser's hclk failure is the fabric fallback (section 4).  The
  discriminating test is cheap and offline-ish: build a variant that keeps the
  chaser but drives only the original five lanes, leaving IO5/6/7 unconsumed.
  If `hclk` returns, the extra driven lanes are the disturbance; if it does not,
  the chaser logic is.  Either answer narrows what the fabric fallback is
  sensitive to, and neither needs a new question asked of the user.
- Whether the three blink rates of the pre-chaser design were 2-3x apart, as
  the counters and the doubled divider rates imply, or as similar as the user
  first read them.  The chaser does not answer this and does not need to; it is
  recorded as open in `clock-smoke-led-and-uart.txt` section 4 and stays open.
- The display, which is what all of this is for.  Sections 18 and 19 of
  `desktop-clock-routing.txt` remain the handoff, and section 4 above is now the
  best instrument for the half of it that concerns the clock.
